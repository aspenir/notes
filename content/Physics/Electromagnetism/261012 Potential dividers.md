A circuit that splits an input [[260914 Electric potential|voltage]] $V_{in}$ between components to produce a specific output voltage $V_{out}$ across a given component is called a _potential divider_.
 ![InkDrawing](<Ink/Drawing/2026.10.12 - 9.52am.svg>) [Edit Drawing](https://youtu.be/2arL1jh8ihA?type=inkDrawing&width=500&aspectRatio=1.778&viewBoxX=0&viewBoxY=0&viewBoxW=2000&viewBoxH=1125)
An external circuit/appliance is connected in [[261005 Kirchhoff's Laws#Resistance in parallel|parallel]] with the divider.

This is the core principle of how potentiometers and rheostats work - the sliding terminal effectively splits the resistance track into two resistors with a terminal in the middle, allowing for $V_{out}=\frac{l_{1}}{l_{\Sigma}}$.

### Derivation
We know that the sum of voltages around a closed loop must be equal to $\epsilon$. This means that the "load" of the divider's voltage must equal that of $R_{2}$, which is itself determined by the resistance of $R_{1}$, due to Ohm's law and the fact that voltages sum in series.

### Uses
Aside from letting us produce esoteric potential differences, potential dividers also allow us to produce voltages dependent on the resistances of photoconductors or thermoconductors for sensing circuits.
