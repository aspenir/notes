## Basic proof notation
- $A'$ "not" indicates the inversion of statement $A$
- $\implies$ "implies" indicates a hypothesis between two statements. If $A \implies B$, then if $A$ is true, $B$ is true. It does _not_ state $A$ is true. It is not the same to state $B\implies A$ (the converse statement), as in the first $B$ may be true without $A$ being necessary. It _is_ logically equivalent to write $B' \implies A'$ (the contrapositive statement), but not $A'\implies B'$, which is the contrapositive of the converse. An example of this is the statement $x=1\implies x^{2}=1$. This is true, but the converse ($x^{2}=1\implies x=1$) is _not_ true.
- $\impliedby$ "implied by" is the reverse of $\implies$
- $\iff$ "if and only if", or "iff", is a combination of $\implies$ and $\impliedby$. If $A\iff B$, if $A$ is true, $B$ is true. Again, it does _not_ state either statement is true. An example of this is $x=1\iff x^3=1$.
- $\therefore$ "therefore" indicates the conclusion of a hypothesis. If $A \therefore B$, $A$ is true and therefore the necessary statement $B$ is true. It is analogous to stating $A\land (A\implies B)$. An example of this is $x^3=1\therefore x=1$.
- $\because$ "because" is the reverse of $\therefore$
- $\equiv$ "equivalent to", or "congruent to". In algebra, it provides a distinction between statements like $x^{2}=x\times x$, which is true for all $x$, and $x^{2}=1$, which is only true for some $x$.
- $\exists$ "there exists" states there is a value that satisfies some condition.
- $\forall$ "for all"
- \# "contradiction" (used in proof by contradiction)

$$\forall x \space\exists y \mid y=x$$

### Types of proof
There are many types of proof:
- Proof by deduction (or direct proof) - using known truths to prove a hypothesis
- Proof by exhaustion - taking a seemingly infinite (yet finite) number of cases, and proving each one in turn
- Proof by contradiction - assuming a hypothesis is false and proving that this leads to a logical contradiction - a "reductio ad absurdum"
- Proof by induction - proving an initial case, and then proving this follows to the next case, and then proving this continues to infinity

### Examples:
##### 1. Prove that the sum of three consecutive integers is a multiple of 3

Let the least integer in the sum be $n$, and the next two therefore $n+1$, $n+2$. The sum of these is:











$$
\begin{aligned}
(n)+(n+1)+(n+2)&=3n+3 \\
&=3(n+1)
\end{aligned}
$$











which has a factor of 3.

##### 2. Prove that the difference of consecutive odd squares is divisible by 8

Odd numbers are of the form $2n-1, n\in \mathbb{Z}$. The next odd number is $2n-1+2$, or $2n+1$. The difference of these is:











$$
\begin{aligned}
(2n+1)^{2}-(2n-1)^{2}&=(2n+1+2n-1)(2n+1-(2n-1)) \\
&=4n(2) \\
&=8n
\end{aligned}
$$











##### 3. Prove that $n^{2}$ even $\implies n$ even

We begin by proving the contrapositive - $n$ not even $\implies n^{2}$ not even. Let $n=2k+1,k\in \mathbb{Z}$:











$$
\begin{aligned}
(2k+1)^{2}&=(2k)^{2}+2(2k)+1 \\
&=4k^{2}+4k+1 \\
&=2(2k(k+1))+1
\end{aligned}
$$











This is clearly odd, completing the proof.

##### 4. Let $n\in \mathbb{N}$. Prove that $(n+1)^{3}>3^n$ if $n\in \{ 1,2,3,4 \}$.

We consider each case:
$n\triangleq 1$:











$$
\begin{aligned}
LHS:(1+1)^{3} &= 2^{3} \\
&=8
 \\
RHS:3^1&=3 \\
 \\
8&>3
\end{aligned}
$$











$n\triangleq 2$:











$$
\begin{aligned}
LHS:(1+2)^{3}&=3^3 \\
&=27 \\
RHS:3^2&=9 \\
 \\
27>9
\end{aligned}
$$











$n\triangleq 3$:











$$
\begin{aligned}
LHS:(3+1)^{3}&=4^3 \\
&=64 \\
RHS:3^3&=27 \\
 \\
64&>27
\end{aligned}
$$











$n\triangleq 4$:











$$
\begin{aligned}
LHS: (4+1)^{3}&=5^3 \\
&=125 \\
RHS:3^5&=81 \\
 \\
125&>81
\end{aligned}
$$











$\therefore(n+1)^{3}>3^n \text{ when } n\in \{ 1,2,3,4 \}$

##### 5. Prove that $n^7-n$ has a factor of 7 for all $n\in \mathbb{Z}$:
There are 7 possible remainders when dividing $n$ by 7, so we may prove by exhaustion.











$$
\begin{aligned}
n^7-n&\equiv n(n^6-1) \\
&\equiv n(n^3-1)(n^3+1)\hspace{1.5cm}(\text{difference of two squares}) \\
\end{aligned}
$$











This has three factors: $n, n^3-1, n^{3}+1$.
Let $n=7k+r,k\in \mathbb{Z},r\in \{ 0,1,2,3,4,5,6 \}$. We need to consider $n^{3}$.











$$
\begin{aligned}
n^{3}&=(7k+r)^{3} \\
&=7^{3}k^{3}+3(7^{2})k^{2}r+3(7)kr^{2}+r^{3}
\end{aligned}
$$











It is obvious to see that all factors except $r^3$ have a factor of 7, so the remainder of $r^3$ when divided by 7 is the same as $n^3$.

So therefore for each value of $r$:
$r=0$: $n$ is a multiple of $7$
$r=1$: $r^3=1$   $\pmod{r^3}=1$  $(n^3-1)$ is a multiple of 7
$r=2:$ $r^3=8$   $\pmod{r^3}=1$  $(n^{3}-1)$ is a multiple of 7
$r=3$: $r^3=27$  $\pmod{r^3}=6$  $(n^{3}+1)$ is a multiple of 7
$r=4$: $r^3=64$  $\pmod{r^3}=1$  $(n^3-1)$ is a multiple of 7
$r=5$: $r^3=125$ $\pmod{r^3}=6$  $(n^3+1)$ is a multiple of 7
$r=6$: $r^3=216$ $\pmod{r^3}=6$  $(n^3+1)$ is a multiple of 7

##### 6. Prove that, if $x=n^{2}+5n+29$, it is not true that $x$ is prime for all values of $n$.
We may prove this by counterexample, $n=29$. 
$$x=29^{2}+5(29)+29=29(29+5+1)=29(35)$$
Which has two non-trivial factors, and is thus composite.

##### 7. Prove that there are an infinite number of primes
We begin by assuming that there are _not_. Let this finite set of primes be $p_1,p_{2}\dots p_{n}$.

Now let $m=p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}\dots p_{n}+1$ (or $m=1+\prod_{r=1}^np_{r})$, and consider factors of $m$. $\forall p, m\bmod p = 1\implies m\nmid p$. Thus either $m$ is prime, or it has some other prime factor not in this set. Therefore, there must be another prime. This continues ad infinitum quod erat demonstrandum.

##### 8. Prove that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational
We begin by assuming that $\sqrt{ 2 }$ is _rational_.
$$\sqrt{ 2 } = \frac{a}{b}, a\in \mathbb{Z},b\in \mathbb{N}$$











$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{a}{b}=\sqrt{ 2 } \\
\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}=2 \\
a^{2}=2b^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$











The right hand side has a factor of 2, so therefore $a^{2}$ must be even (and thus $a$ must be even). We will then express $a$ as $2k, k\in \mathbb{Z}$ and substitute back in:











$$
\begin{aligned}
2b^{2}&=(2k)^{2} \\
&=4k^{2} \\
b^{2}&=2k^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$











Therefore $b^{2}$ must also have a factor of 2 (and so does $b$).
This means $a$ and $b$ are not coprime #, and so $\sqrt{2}$ cannot be rational.
