## Set Notation
Sets can be most simply represented by listing their elements e.g.
$$Y=\{ A,3,\text{computer}, 56,\text{battery}\}$$
This set has 5 elements, or $n(Y)=4$ or $|Y|=4$.

The empty set, $\emptyset$, has 0 elements, whilst the universal set, $\mathcal{E}$ has infinite elements.

However, often sets can be infinite, or have too many elements to list. We define these using _set builder notation_.

$$S=\{ s\in \mathbb{N}:s\text{ is not prime} \}$$
Here $S$ is the set of elements in another set, $\mathbb{N}$, which are not prime. For any given set $X$, $X'$ is its complement, or the set of elements *not* in $X$.

Since $S$ is fully composed of elements in $\mathbb{N}$ (and is not equal to $\mathbb{N}$, we can write
$$S\subset \mathbb{N}$$

Two sets $A$ and $B$ may comprise some elements that appear in both - we can express this as the _intersection_ of $A$ and $B$ - $A\cap B$. We can express the set containing _all_ elements of $A$ and $B$ as the union - $A\cup B$.

We can also express sets using _interval notation_ - square brackets denote a closed interval and traditional brackets denote an open interval.











$$
\begin{aligned}
[3,5]&=\{ x:3\leq x\leq5 \} \\
[3,5)&=\{ x:3\leq x<5 \} \\
(3,5]&=\{ x:3<x\leq 5 \} \\
(3,5)&=\{ x:3<x<5 \} \\
 \\
A\triangleq(-\infty,3]&=\{ x:x\leq3 \} \\
B\triangleq[3,\infty)&=\{ x:x\geq3 \} \\\\\\\\ \\
A\cup B &=(-\infty,\infty)=\{ x:x\in \mathbb{R} \} \\
A\cap B &=\{3\}

\end{aligned}
$$











## Types of Numbers
The natural numbers, $\mathbb{N}$, are the "traditional" numbers:











$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{N}&=\{ 1,2,3,4\dots \} \\
\mathbb{N}_{0}&=\mathbb{N}\cap \{0\}
\end{aligned}
$$











The integers, $\mathbb{Z}$, include -1 and its multiples:











$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{Z}&=\{\dots{,}-2,-1,0,1,2\dots\} \\
\mathbb{N}&\subset\mathbb{Z}
\end{aligned}
$$











The rational numbers, $\mathbb{Q}$, include fractional values:











$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{Q} &= \left\{  \frac{a}{b} : a \in \mathbb{Z},b\in\mathbb{N}  \right\} \\
\mathbb{N}&\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset \mathbb{Q}
\end{aligned}
$$











The real numbers, $\mathbb{R}$, include irrational numbers (such as radicals and transcendental numbers):











$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{R}&=\{ x: -\infty \leq x \leq \infty \} \\
\mathbb{N}&\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}
\end{aligned}
$$











_A number is transcendental over a set if it cannot be the solution of a polynomial with coefficients from the set. For example, $\pi$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$._
